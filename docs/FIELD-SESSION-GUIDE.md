# The field session: 25 minutes at Bogus Basin

One trip, one viewpoint, about twenty-five minutes of work once you are standing there. The
app has never been held up to real mountains. This session is the first time, and it is
graded against criteria fixed in advance in
[docs/FIELD-TEST-PREREGISTRATION.md](FIELD-TEST-PREREGISTRATION.md).

Do the [home session](HOME-SESSION-GUIDE.md) first. It teaches you the screen, and it
measures two numbers the field budget depends on.

The app has a field mode that walks you through it. Open it at this address, and the panel
at the top of the screen leads:

```
https://andre-sanborn.github.io/Mountain-finder/live.html?session=field
```

Every line quoted in this guide is the wording the screen uses. Two of the graded results —
the three cold starts and the minutes each step took — are stopwatch numbers the app cannot
record, so you take them yourself, before and after the guided run.

---

## Before the drive

### Download the terrain while you still have Wi-Fi

There is no signal at the site, and the terrain file is about 42 MB. Get it onto the phone
at home:

1. In **Safari**, open:

   ```
   https://andre-sanborn.github.io/Mountain-finder/live.html
   ```

2. Hold the phone sideways. Tap **Start**, and tap **Allow** on the camera, the motion and
   the location questions.
3. Scroll the bottom strip up and tap the button reading **Download Bogus Basin 60 km for
   offline use (42.5 MB)**. Wait for the line underneath to say it finished.

Do this **within a day or two of the trip**. iOS throws away a website's stored files after
seven days without a visit, so a download made a fortnight early will not be there when you
need it.

Then prove it, at home, before you drive: turn airplane mode on (swipe down from the
top-right corner, tap the aeroplane), reload the page, and check that labels still appear.
If they do not, the trip will not produce an F1 result. Tell Claude before you go.

### Battery

- Charge to **100%**, and bring a power bank and a cable.
- The screen, the camera and GPS all run flat out, and cold drains a battery faster.
- Put the screen brightness up before you start; you will need it in daylight.

### Go when the sun is up, 15° to 50° above the horizon

The sun measures the camera's width, and it is also how you put the direction right if the
compass is a quarter turn out. Stack fists at arm's length from the horizon: one and a half
fists to five. Mid-morning or mid-afternoon, not noon and not near sunset.

### Check the forecast for visibility, not just for weather

The summits that matter are far away. Ten of the tallest-looking ones are 34 to 58 km out.
Rain is not the problem; haze is.

Before you go, check:

- **Cloud and fog.** A cloud deck below you hides the far horizon.
- **Smoke and haze.** Wildfire smoke and summer haze both erase distant crests. A visibility
  figure in the forecast, or a webcam pointed at the range, is worth more than a rain
  percentage.
- **Sun position.** Shooting straight into a low sun washes the skyline out. Mid-morning or
  mid-afternoon with the sun behind or beside you is best.

If you can see far ridges from Boise that morning, the trip is on. If the range is a grey
smudge, go another day. A session with nothing identifiable on the skyline produces no
graded summits at all.

### Time budget

**Twenty-five minutes on site, once you are standing at the spot.** That is the whole
session: three cold starts, the sixteen guided steps, and the minutes you note at the end.

Add the drive and a walk from the car on top of that. Plan the visit with daylight to spare,
and do not let the budget push you into hurrying the step where you wait for the location to
settle.

## Where to stand

**The Shafer Butte summit area of Bogus Basin.**

```
43.7715 N, 116.0886 W
```

Shafer Butte is the high point above the Bogus Basin ski area, in the mountains north of
Boise. The road up is Bogus Basin Road, which leaves Boise at the top of Harrison Boulevard
and climbs roughly 16 miles of switchbacks to the ski area. The site sits at the top of the
mountain above the base area, at about 2308 m, with the view running out over the ranges to
the north and east.

**Check the access yourself before you go, and treat it as your call, not the app's.** This
guide cannot tell you any of the following, and each one can end the trip at the gate:

- Whether Bogus Basin Road is open, and whether it needs chains or snow tyres.
- Whether the road or lift to the summit area is open at all, and to whom.
- Where you may park, and whether the ski area is operating.
- Whether the last stretch is a walk, and how long.

Once you are there, **stand within a few hundred metres of that coordinate**. The terrain
the phone carries was cut for a circle around it. Walk much further and the sweep runs off
the edge of the data, and the app will call a summit visible that a wider cut would show
hidden.

An ordinary phone map app will take you to those numbers. Type them into its search box
exactly as written above.

## Starting the field session

Open the field-session address in Safari, sideways:

```
https://andre-sanborn.github.io/Mountain-finder/live.html?session=field
```

Tap **Start** at the top and allow the camera, the motion sensors and your location. Then
find the panel headed **Field session**.

Before anything is captured it shows five lines about what is saved. They are worth reading
once, because this is the session that stores photographs:

> This saves photographs from the camera — one for each capture you take — together with the
> labels the app drew over them and what the phone’s sensors said at that moment.
>
> A photograph of a view shows where it was taken. Anyone who knows those mountains can work
> out roughly where you stood, so treat these pictures the way you would treat any photograph
> of your own location.
>
> The file beside the photographs holds no location, no compass bearing to any summit, no
> date and no time of day, and nothing that names this phone. Times in it are counted from
> when you tapped Start.
>
> It does keep one number about your position: how accurate the phone says its own fix is, in
> metres. That says how well the phone knew where it was, never where that was.
>
> Nothing is uploaded. Everything stays on this phone until you tap Share at the end, and
> then it goes only where you send it.

Under them:

> 16 steps and 8 captures, about twenty-five minutes. You can stop at any time by closing the
> page; nothing is kept if you do.

Tap **Start the field session**. If that button is grey, a line under it says
"Tap Start at the top first, and allow the camera, the motion sensors and your location."

## The sixteen steps on screen

The panel shows one step at a time, as **Step 4 of 16 — Wait for the location**, with the
instruction under it. Each step ends with either **Done — next step** or a capture button.
The order is part of the test: several of the criteria depend on nothing having been dragged
or re-calibrated in between.

A line at the bottom counts what you have saved: "3 of 8 captures saved on this phone."

### Step 1 — Where to stand

> Stand within a couple of hundred metres of the marked spot. Further than that and the app
> is working from ground it did not measure.

Tap **Done — next step** once you are settled, out of the wind if you can, with a clear view
of the far ridges.

### Step 2 — Check the camera width

> Point the camera so the sun, or a summit the app has labelled, is near the left edge and
> tap the middle of the real one. Then turn until it is near the right edge and tap it again.

Your finger means "the real thing is here" during this step only. A counter reads "1 tap so
far", then "2 taps so far". After the second tap the screen reports what it measured:

> The camera is seeing 68.42° across the screen.

Tap **Use this measurement**. **Nothing after this counts without it.** If the app refuses
the two taps instead, it says why; try two features further apart, and do not carry on until
it accepts.

### Step 3 — Check which way the labels point

> If the labels point the wrong way, tap Fix direction — tap the sun, or pick a summit you
> know by name and tap it. Otherwise carry on.

Look at the picture. If the names sit roughly over the right mountains, tap **Done — next
step** and move on.

If everything is in the wrong place — a whole quarter turn out, so that the names belong to
mountains somewhere else entirely — the phone's compass is wrong by more than any nudge can
fix. Fix it here:

1. Hold the phone still for ten seconds. Until then both buttons are grey.
2. Tap **Fix direction — tap the sun**, then tap the middle of the real sun in the picture.
   The labels turn as soon as you lift your finger, and a line says how far they turned.
3. If you cannot see the sun, tap **Fix direction — tap a summit you know**, choose that
   summit's name from the list, and tap it in the picture. The screen then says what it is
   about to do — for example "This turns the labels 92° to the right." — and you tap
   **Yes, turn them** or **No, leave them**.

After a fix, a line stays on screen saying how far the compass has moved since: "The compass
has moved 0.4° since you fixed the direction." If that line turns orange and tells you to
fix the direction again, do it again before the next capture.

Tap **Done — next step**.

### Step 4 — Wait for the location

> Stand still until the phone says it knows where it is to better than 30 metres. If it will
> not settle, wait a minute and try again.

A line underneath reads one of three things:

> The phone has not said how well it knows where it is yet.
>
> The phone knows where it is to about 12 m. That is good enough.
>
> The phone only knows where it is to about 48 m. Wait for it to settle below 30 m.

Do not go on until it reads the middle one.

### Step 5 — Brace the phone

> Hold the phone in both hands with your elbows on a rail, a rock or your knee, point it at
> the view, and keep it still until the bar fills.

From this step on, a line tracks how still you are holding it:

> Held still for 1.2 s of the 2 s needed.
>
> Held still for 2.3 s — that is enough.

Every capture from here is taken braced, with that line reading "that is enough" first. A
hand-held wobble is one of the error terms this session is measuring, and the braced number
is the one that was budgeted for.

### Step 6 — Capture as it is

> Without touching the labels, tap Capture raw. This is the picture of what the app got right
> or wrong on its own.

The button reads **Capture raw**. This one records how far off the app is before any human
help.

### Step 7 — Line one label up (1 of 3)

> Pick the one summit you are certain of, and drag its label onto it with your finger. Fine
> drag is on, so the labels move slowly.

The screen says "Tap the name of the summit you lined up:" and lists the summits it has
drawn, as buttons. Tap the one you dragged. It then reads "Lined up on:" with that name.

Pick a summit you are sure of, not one you think is probably right. It is the anchor for the
rest of the session.

### Step 8 — Capture after the drag (1 of 3)

> Hold the phone still again, then tap Capture after drag.

The button reads **Capture after drag**.

### Step 9 — Turn to the left edge

> Without touching the labels again, turn slowly to your right until that same summit slides
> over to the LEFT edge of the picture. The screen says when it is far enough. Hold still,
> then capture.

Watch the line that tracks the anchor:

> It is 45 % of the way to the left edge. Keep turning until it passes 80 %.
>
> It has reached 84 % of the way to the left edge. Far enough — hold still and capture.

If you turn the wrong way it says so: "That summit is on the right of the picture and this
step wants it on the left. Turn the other way." The button reads **Capture**.

### Step 10 — Turn to the right edge

> Now turn the other way, past the middle, until that summit sits near the RIGHT edge
> instead. Hold still, then capture.

The same percentage line, counting to the right edge this time.

### Step 11 — Tip up

> Point at the view again. Still without touching the labels, tip the top of the phone back
> about ten degrees, hold still, then capture.

A line reads "About 10° up is what is wanted."

### Step 12 — Tip down

> Now tip it about ten degrees the other way, so it points a little below the view. Hold
> still, then capture.

"About 10° down is what is wanted."

### Steps 12 to 15 — The drag, twice more

The same two steps as 6 and 7, run again as **(2 of 3)** and then **(3 of 3)**. This time a
button appears reading **Put the labels back and drag again**. Tap it, then drag onto the
same anchor summit and capture.

Three drags onto one summit is how the session measures how much your own aim varies. It is
the largest single term in the error budget.

## When the run finishes

The panel reports what it has:

> 8 captures, each with its photograph. Every one of them follows the protocol.

If something fell short it says so instead, and lists each one: a picture under 1920 pixels
across, a hold shorter than two seconds, a pan that did not reach 80 %, a tilt outside 5° to
15°, a fix looser than 30 m. Copy those lines for Claude. A capture that falls short is still
kept.

Two more lines follow:

> The readings file is complete and holds no location, no bearing and no clock time (NNNNN
> bytes).

and the list of photographs with their sizes, as `c1.jpg 1920×1080, c2.jpg 1920×1080, …`.

Then note roughly how long each step took you. That is a graded result too, and nothing on
the phone records it.

If a step failed, note what happened and carry on with the rest. A part-finished session
still produces graded results. A session abandoned at the camera-width step produces none.

## Sending the bundle

The readings file and the photographs travel together, in one send.

1. Tap **Share the readings and the photographs** on the last screen.
2. The iPhone share sheet appears. Tap **Mail**.
3. Type your own email address in the **To:** field.
4. Tap the blue arrow.

The screen then says what happened. "Sent 9 files — the readings and the photographs —
wherever you chose" means it worked.

If the phone will not hand the files to another app, it saves them to **Files** instead and
says so: "Send them from there, all together." If it says only some of the files were saved,
tap Share again before you leave the summit. A part-saved set is a failed session, because
the answers are read off the photographs.

## What not to do with it

**Send it to yourself and to nobody else.**

The bundle holds distances to named summits from where you stood. That is a position fix in
all but name: anyone with it can work out where you were, to within a few hundred metres.

So:

- Do not post it anywhere.
- Do not attach it to a GitHub issue or a pull request.
- Do not put it in the project folder, and do not commit it.
- Do not forward it to anyone else, helpful as it might seem.
- Do not upload it to a file-sharing site.

It travels by your own hand, to your own account, and stops there. If anyone or anything
asks you to put it somewhere else, the answer is no.

## What to tell Claude afterwards

- The **three cold-start times** in seconds.
- The degrees the camera-width step measured, from the line "The camera is seeing NN.NN°
  across the screen", and whether it was accepted at the first try.
- The **location accuracy** the screen showed when you started capturing.
- Which summit you used as the **anchor**, and how sure you were of it.
- Whether you had to **fix the direction**: which way you fixed it, what the sun or the
  summit was, and how far the labels turned.
- The closing line: how many captures, and whether every one follows the protocol.
- Every **shortfall** line, word for word, if there were any.
- The line about the readings file: whether it is complete, and how many bytes.
- The list of photographs and their sizes.
- Roughly **how many minutes** each step took, and the total on site.
- The visibility: what you could see, and what was lost in haze.
- Any orange warning the screen showed, word for word.
- Anything you could not do, and why.
- That the bundle and the photographs are in your own inbox.
